The front door is the one part of a house that gets judged in complete isolation. Nobody sees your kitchen worktop before they've decided how they feel about your home — but the door is the first thing, often the only thing, a visitor or a buyer or a passer-by actually looks at. That disproportion is exactly why the colour decision feels so much harder than painting any other single surface in the house, and why most people either freeze and repaint it the same safe colour it already was, or chase whatever shade is trending that year without checking if it suits the building it's attached to.

It does suit some buildings and not others. A colour that looks editorial on a Georgian townhouse can look like a mistake on a 1990s semi. This guide works through how to actually choose — starting with the house, not the paint chart.

---

## Read your house before you read a colour chart

Before any colour decision, look at three things you already have: the brick or render tone, the roof colour, and the era of the building. These three are fixed. The door colour is the only variable, which means its job is to work with what's already there, not to be chosen on its own merits.

Warm red or orange-toned brick pulls naturally toward greens, blues and black — colours with enough depth to hold their own against a warm wall without fighting it. Pale render or stone pulls toward almost anything, which is both a gift and a trap: with no strong undertone to react against, it's easy to choose a colour that's simply wrong for the house's era rather than wrong for its materials. Grey or buff brick is the hardest base to work with — it has a cooler undertone than most people register at a glance, and warm colours like terracotta can look slightly muddy against it where they'd sing against red brick.

The roof matters more than people expect. A dark slate or charcoal roof creates a visual anchor that a black or deep navy door echoes naturally. A warm terracotta tile roof does the same for a terracotta or burnt-orange door. When the door colour picks up a tone already present somewhere else on the building, the whole facade reads as considered rather than decorated.

## Finish and hardware

<div style="margin: 1.75rem 0; border: 1px solid #E8D8A0; overflow: hidden;">
  <div style="background: #C8952A; padding: 0.4rem 1.25rem;">
    <span style="color: #FFF8E7; font-size: 0.65rem; font-weight: 700; letter-spacing: 0.12em; text-transform: uppercase;">Watch out for</span>
  </div>
  <div style="padding: 1rem 1.25rem; background: #FEFAED; font-size: 0.875rem; color: #6B5420; line-height: 1.65;">A south-facing door takes far more direct UV exposure than any other painted surface on the house. Standard interior-grade paint, or even budget exterior paint, will chalk and fade within a year in strong sun. Pay for a paint specifically rated for UV stability on doors that face south or west, and expect to repaint roughly every five to seven years regardless of quality.</div>
</div>

Satin or eggshell is the standard finish for a reason — it has enough sheen to wipe clean but doesn't show every brushstroke and surface imperfection the way full gloss does. Full gloss looks striking in good light but is unforgiving of an imperfect surface underneath, and shows dust and water spots far more readily.

Hardware finish should echo something else in the colour scheme rather than being chosen in isolation. Brass against a dark door reads warm and traditional. Matte black against a pale render reads contemporary and quiet. Chrome is the most neutral option and the easiest to get wrong — it can read cold against a warm-toned door and brick combination, so test it in person before committing to a full hardware set.

---

The right front door colour does two jobs at once: it has to hold its own as a focal point and it has to look like it was always meant to be there, not added afterward. Start from the brick, the render, the roof and the era of the house rather than from a colour you've seen elsewhere, test it properly in your own light, and use a paint rated for the material and the exposure your door actually gets. Get those three things right and almost any colour on this list will work — get them wrong and even the most expensive designer paint won't save it.
