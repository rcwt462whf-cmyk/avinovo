You repainted the walls. You bought new cushions. You rearranged the furniture and added a plant in the corner. The room looks different — objectively better — and yet something is still wrong. It does not feel the way the inspiration photo felt. It reads as clinical, or flat, or vaguely uncomfortable in a way that is difficult to name. You assume the paint colour was slightly off, or the sofa fabric is not quite right, and start considering whether to redo the whole thing.

The problem is almost certainly not the paint, the furniture, or the layout. It is the light bulbs. Specifically, it is a single number printed on every bulb box that most people never look at — the colour temperature, measured in Kelvin — and it is the most common reason a redecorated room fails to feel the way it should.

## What colour temperature actually means

Every light source has a colour. Not a dramatic, obvious colour like red or blue, but a subtle tint that shifts everything in the room towards warmth or coolness. This is measured in Kelvin (K), and the scale runs from around 1800 K (deep amber, like a candle) up to 6500 K and beyond (harsh blue-white, like midday sun or a hospital corridor).

The scale is counterintuitive. Lower numbers are warmer. Higher numbers are cooler. This trips people up because we associate "higher" with "more" and assume a higher Kelvin number means more light or better light. It does not. It means bluer light.

The vast majority of bulbs sold in European supermarkets and hardware shops sit between 4000 K and 6500 K. They are labelled "daylight" or "cool white" or simply "bright" — words that sound desirable when you are standing in an aisle trying to light your home. They are not desirable. They are the reason your room feels like an office.

## Why the wrong Kelvin ruins a room even after redecorating

This is not a subtle effect. Colour temperature changes everything in a room — not just the mood, but how surfaces, fabrics, and paint colours physically appear.

A wall painted in Farrow & Ball Elephants Breath — a warm greige that reads as sophisticated and enveloping in every interiors magazine — will look grey and slightly dirty under 5000 K light. The warm undertone that makes the colour work is suppressed by the blue cast of the bulb, and what remains is a flat, cold version of the colour you chose. You will assume you picked the wrong shade. You did not. You lit it wrong.

The same applies to fabrics. A linen sofa in oatmeal or sand will look warm and textured under 2700 K. Under 4000 K, it looks washed out — the yellow and cream tones flatten and the fabric reads as beige in the least appealing sense of the word. Wood floors shift from honey to grey-brown. Terracotta tiles lose their warmth entirely. Every material that relies on warm undertones — which is most materials used in residential interiors — is undermined by cool light.

## The mixed-Kelvin problem

There is something worse than having all cool white bulbs, and that is having a mix. One lamp at 2700 K, the ceiling light at 4000 K, and a desk lamp at 5000 K creates a visual confusion that your brain registers as discomfort without being able to name the cause. Different areas of the room have different colour casts. Surfaces near the warm lamp look golden; the same surface under the ceiling light looks grey. Shadows take on competing tints.

This happens more often than you would expect, because people buy bulbs one at a time, from different shops, often without checking the Kelvin rating. Over the course of a year, a living room can accumulate three or four different colour temperatures, each creating its own micro-climate of tint. The room never looks coherent, and no amount of accessorising will fix it.

The rule is absolute: every bulb in a living space should be the same colour temperature. For living rooms, bedrooms, and dining rooms, that temperature is 2700 K. Not a range, not "around 3000 K" — 2700 K, every bulb, every lamp, without exception. For kitchens and bathrooms, 3000 K is acceptable. For everything else in the home, 2700 K.

## How to check what you currently have

This takes five minutes and costs nothing.

Walk through each room in the evening with the lights on. Look at the bulb boxes if you still have them, or remove a bulb and check the printed specifications on the base or the glass — nearly all LED bulbs have the Kelvin rating printed directly on them. Write down the rating for each fixture.

If you find a mix — and you almost certainly will — replace the outliers. A multipack of quality 2700 K LED bulbs (E27 or B22 base, depending on your fixtures) costs between €8 and €15 for four. This is the cheapest change you can make to a room and often the most impactful.

## What about "tunable white" and smart bulbs?

Smart bulbs that allow you to adjust colour temperature between warm and cool (typically 2200 K to 6500 K) are genuinely useful — but only if you set them once and leave them. The appeal of being able to shift from warm to cool throughout the day sounds logical in theory. In practice, most people set them up, experiment for a day, and then never touch the setting again. If you do buy tuneable bulbs, set the evening scene to 2700 K, set a daytime scene to 3000 K at most, and leave them on a schedule or automation.

The trap with smart bulbs is the opposite of the mixed-Kelvin problem: they make it easy to accidentally set each bulb to a slightly different temperature. If you have five smart bulbs in a room and each was last adjusted to a different Kelvin via the app, the visual effect is the same incoherent mess described above. Set them all identically and group them so they always change together.

The cost premium is significant. A standard 2700 K LED costs €2–€4. A tuneable smart bulb from Philips Hue or IKEA TRÅDFRI costs €12–€25 per bulb plus the hub. Unless you specifically want automation or voice control, the standard LED at 2700 K does the same job for a fraction of the price.

## Room by room: what actually works

**Living room:** 2700 K everywhere. No exceptions. This is the room where warmth matters most, and where cool light does the most damage. If you have a single ceiling fixture, replace the bulb and add a dimmer — dropping to 30–40% intensity in the evening transforms the room more than any furniture change.

**Bedroom:** 2700 K, or 2200 K for bedside lamps if you want the candlelight effect. The bedroom is the one room where going slightly warmer than 2700 K genuinely improves the atmosphere. A 2200 K bulb in a bedside lamp creates a reading light that does not suppress melatonin as aggressively as brighter, cooler alternatives.

**Kitchen:** 3000 K for general lighting, particularly under-cabinet task lighting where you need to see food colours accurately. The worktop area benefits from slightly cooler light; the dining end of a kitchen-diner should drop back to 2700 K if possible.

**Bathroom:** 3000 K for the main light. Avoid going higher — a 4000 K bathroom light makes skin look grey and unflattering, which is the last thing you want in the room where you get ready in the morning.

**Home office:** This is the one space where cooler light has a genuine argument. 3000–4000 K supports alertness and concentration during working hours. If your office is also a living space in the evening, a tuneable smart bulb set to 4000 K during the day and 2700 K in the evening is one of the few cases where the smart bulb premium genuinely pays for itself.

The next time a room feels wrong after redecorating — flat, cold, unwelcoming, not quite like the photo — check the bulbs before you blame the paint. Walk through the house with a phone torch as your only light, then turn on each lamp one at a time and notice the tint. If anything reads blue, grey, or clinical, the Kelvin is too high. Replace it with 2700 K, and the room you already built will finally look the way you intended it to.
