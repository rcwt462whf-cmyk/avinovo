Hardware is the one finish decision in a kitchen or bathroom that gets touched constantly and considered last. People spend weeks choosing a worktop and a paint colour, then pick the cabinet handles in the final twenty minutes of an order, usually defaulting to whatever finish the worktop or tap is already in. That's backwards, because hardware is handled more times per day than almost any other surface in the house — and the finish you choose determines whether your cabinets look better or worse after two years of actual hands on them.

This guide compares five finishes across the things that actually matter once the photos are taken and real life starts.

Lacquered brass is real brass sealed under a clear protective coating, which keeps the polished shine fixed in place and stops the metal from reacting to air and moisture underneath. It is the brass finish that looks the same on day one and year three — which is exactly its strength and its limitation.

The lacquer is also the weak point. It can scratch, chip at edges that get knocked or gripped hard, and once it fails in one spot the brass underneath begins tarnishing unevenly, leaving a patchy finish that looks like a fault rather than a deliberate patina. Once lacquer starts failing, the only real fix is stripping and refinishing the whole piece — there is no partial repair.

Cost: typically €12–30 per handle for a mid-range lacquered brass pull, putting a full kitchen of 20–30 pieces in the €300–800 range before knobs.

Unlacquered brass is bare metal with no protective coating, which means it tarnishes and ages on contact with air, moisture and skin oils from day one. This is the entire point. The areas that get touched most — the underside of a pull, the edge gripped daily — polish themselves bright through use, while the rest of the piece slowly deepens to an antique gold. No two unlacquered brass kitchens age the same way, because the pattern of wear is unique to how that specific kitchen gets used.

There is genuinely nothing to fail here, because there is no coating to chip. A scratch on unlacquered brass is just more brass — it can be polished out entirely, or left to tarnish back into the surrounding patina within weeks. This is the only hardware finish on this list that is effectively unbreakable in the way that matters for daily kitchen use.

The trade-off is acceptance, not maintenance. If you want every handle in the kitchen to look identical and unchanged five years from now, unlacquered brass is the wrong choice — it is designed to look different, on purpose, the longer you live with it.

Cost: typically €18–45 per handle, slightly above lacquered brass because the unlacquered, hand-finished product is usually positioned as the premium tier by most manufacturers.

Matte black is the most versatile finish made today — it works against literally every worktop colour, every cabinet tone and every wall colour without a single clash, which is why it has become the default safe choice for kitchens that haven't settled on a strong material palette yet.

The real-world problem with matte black is fingerprints, and it is a bigger problem than most people expect before living with it. The matte, slightly absorbent coating shows oil and skin contact far more visibly than a polished or brushed finish would, especially in direct light. A black pull on a frequently used cabinet will need wiping down every few days to look as clean as the day it was installed — this is the most common hardware regret reported by people one year into a renovation.

Quality varies enormously by manufacturer. Budget matte black coatings wear through at edges and high-contact points within a year or two, revealing the base metal underneath in a way that reads as cheap rather than intentionally distressed. A genuine powder-coated or PVD finish costs more but holds its colour for the life of the kitchen.

Cost: budget matte black runs €5–12 per handle; a genuine PVD-coated finish that actually holds up runs €15–35.

Brushed nickel is the practical option nobody gets excited about and almost nobody regrets. The fine directional texture of the brushing physically scatters light and disguises fingerprints, water spots and light scratches far better than any polished finish — it is, in genuinely practical terms, the lowest-maintenance metal finish available for a kitchen that gets used hard every day.

The look reads as warmer and softer than chrome, with a slight grey-gold undertone that sits more comfortably against warm wood tones and cream cabinetry than chrome's cooler blue-white does. It is not a finish that photographs as dramatically as brass or matte black, which is exactly why it tends to be underrated in design content while remaining one of the most-purchased finishes by people who actually live in their kitchens.

Cost: typically €8–20 per handle, sitting comfortably in the middle of this list on both price and drama.

Chrome is the most durable and least expensive finish on this list, and also the one that shows fingerprints most aggressively of all of them — the high-polish mirror surface that gives chrome its appeal is precisely what makes every fingertip mark visible under any direct light. In a kitchen used by children, this is worth genuinely weighing before committing.

It does not tarnish, fade, or change in any way over decades of use, and a scratch is rare given how hard the chrome plating is. The aesthetic reads as cooler and more clinical than nickel or brass, which suits contemporary and minimalist kitchens far better than traditional or warm-toned ones.

Cost: typically €4–15 per handle, making chrome the most budget-friendly option that still performs well functionally.

If you have small children, a busy household, or simply no patience for wiping fingerprints off cabinet fronts daily, brushed nickel or unlacquered brass will serve you better than the two highest-drama finishes on this list. If you want a kitchen that looks the same on the day you sell the house as it did on the day it was fitted, lacquered brass or chrome are the only two genuinely static options. And if you're drawn to the idea of a kitchen that visibly ages with your family rather than staying frozen, unlacquered brass is the only finish actually designed to do that on purpose.
